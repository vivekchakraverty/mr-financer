# Mr. Mentor business plan questionnaire

Answer in plain language. A rough range is fine. For any number, you can choose **Actual**, **Estimate**, **I don’t know**, or **Doesn’t apply**. Use zero when the amount really is zero. Mr. Mentor will use your answers to draft your plan and show which figures need your review.

## 1. Your business idea

| Question to show the user | Answer field |
| --- | --- |
| **1. What are you selling, and what problem does it solve?** | One or two sentences. Example: “I sell ready-to-cook meal kits to working couples who want dinner in 20 minutes.” Reuse the description from your earlier product or competitor search if you have one. |
| **2. Who is most likely to buy first?** | Describe one group. If the person who pays is different from the person who uses it, tell us who pays. |
| **3. Where will you start selling, and which currency should the plan use?** | City, region, country, or online markets, plus a currency. |
| **4. How far along are you?** | Idea / testing / about to launch / already selling. Add your expected launch month or next expansion month if you know it. |
| **5. What do customers use today, and why would they choose you?** | Name a business, another way of solving the problem, or “I’m still finding out.” Add one reason someone might switch. |
| **6. How will you reach your first customers?** | Pick up to two channels. Examples: referrals, local shops, direct outreach, marketplaces, social media, or ads. |
| **7. Who will run the business, and what would you like to achieve in the next 12 months?** | Example: “I’ll handle sales, my partner will make the product, and we want 20 recurring customers.” Add anything you still need, such as a supplier or a trained employee. If you’ve tested the idea, you can add how many people tried it or paid. |
| **8. Who is this plan for?** | Myself or my team / lender / investor / grant application / other. |

## 2. Prices, costs, and cash

Start with your main offer. Add another offer if it has a different price or cost. Use the same unit for price, cost, sales, and capacity, such as an order, a job, or an active monthly subscription. Show only the numeric fields that fit the way the business earns money.

| Question to show the user | Answer field |
| --- | --- |
| **9. How will you get paid, and how much will you charge?** | Amount plus what it buys. Examples: ₹800 per order, ₹5,000 per job, or ₹399 per subscriber per month. For commissions, enter your percentage and a typical transaction size. If customers use it for free, ask who pays and what they pay for. |
| **10. How many paid orders, jobs, or active subscriptions could you have in an early month and by month 12?** | Two counts or ranges, such as 10–20 and 30–50. An existing business can use its latest typical month as the starting point. If orders vary with the season, indicate the busy and slow months. |
| **11. What does one sale cost you to deliver?** | Amount per order, job, or active subscriber, including costs such as materials, packaging, shipping you cover, contractor time, or payment fees. Let the user enter a total or add line items. |
| **12. What bills do you expect each month, even if you make few sales?** | One monthly total or a short breakdown for items such as rent, staff, software, marketing, and any regular pay you plan to take. If you expect a new hire or cost increase, add the amount and when it starts, if known. |
| **13. What one-time purchases or payments do you need to get started or expand?** | List amounts for items such as equipment, a website, deposits, or first stock. If you’ll pay at different times, add the month for each item you know. |
| **14. How much cash can you put into the business at the start of this plan?** | Amount available for business use. If outside funding is relevant, add the amount, when you expect it, and whether it’s confirmed or still being sought. |
| **15. When do customers pay you, and when do you pay suppliers?** | Ask each separately. Options include upfront, on delivery, or a number of days later. For inventory, optionally ask how much stock you buy ahead and how often. |
| **16. How much can you deliver in a typical month with your current team and equipment?** | Maximum orders, jobs, or active customers served per month. Accept a range or “I don’t know.” |

**Show only when already selling.** Ask for the month or period, recent sales revenue, paid orders or active subscribers, and approximate operating spending. Offer “use last month” or “use an average of the last three months.” Optionally ask what customers owe, what the business owes, and any regular loan payment. For subscriptions, offer optional fields for new paid subscribers and cancellations per month. Ask whether the reported month was typical before using it as a baseline.

**Show only if the forecast needs one more input.** Ask for the missing figure that most changes the result. For example, “You said you can do 12 jobs a month and expect to sell 20. Do you expect to add capacity, or should we start with 12?” If someone hasn’t chosen a price yet, ask whether they have a range they’d like to test. Let them continue with a qualitative plan and a list of missing numbers if they can’t supply usable inputs.

## 3. Optional competitor financials

**Would you like to use financial information from similar businesses?**

- **Choose from Mr. Mentor’s results.** Show the companies from the earlier product or service search. Let users choose the closest matches and look up available filings for those firms using the registries connected to Mr. Mentor. Suggest selecting 1–5 relevant businesses for the plan even if the discovery search found more.
- **Add a company or source.** Enter a company name and country, paste a filing or report link, or upload a PDF or spreadsheet. Ask for a registration ID if more than one company matches.
- **Enter figures I already have.** Let the user enter any available metric. Ask for the source and reporting period, and mark unsourced entries “user-provided, unverified.”
- **Skip for now.** The user can draft the plan and add benchmarks later.

### Review the figures before using them

| Show for each company | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Company and fit | Legal name, country, registry ID where available, and why it’s comparable. Consider customer segment, business model, geography, and scale. |
| Reporting details | Period start and end, original currency and units, and whether figures cover the group, the legal entity, or just a segment. |
| Available figures | Annual revenue, cost of sales or gross profit, operating profit, and net profit where available. Optional balance-sheet items include cash and debt. Record fundraising separately, with the date and type. |
| Evidence | Filing or report link, or uploaded file with page, table, or cell reference. Label each figure as reported, derived, estimated, or unverified. Show “unavailable” where a figure is missing. |

Let users edit or deselect extracted figures. Use comparable published margins to inform a **test range** for the user’s assumptions. Calculate margin only when the numerator and revenue cover the same period and reporting scope. Show the sample size and each company behind a benchmark. Keep figures in their original currency unless the comparison needs conversion, then show the exchange-rate source and date or period. Where only group results or funding announcements are available, explain what the evidence can support.

Example: If a comparable meal-kit company reports a 35% gross margin while the founder’s current estimates imply 50%, the plan can show both and suggest testing which delivery or packaging costs might explain the gap. Use figures only when the underlying cost definitions are comparable.

## 4. How Mr. Mentor generates the plan

1. **Collect and review.** Reuse the earlier idea description and selected competitors. Preserve whether each figure is an actual value, an estimate, a range, unknown, or inapplicable. Ask about contradictions that materially change the plan.
2. **Calculate the numbers in code.** Build the monthly sales, expense, and cash tables from the reviewed inputs. For a simple per-sale business, revenue = price × paid units, and break-even units = monthly fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per sale), rounded up when contribution per sale is positive. For subscriptions use active paying subscribers, and account for new adds and cancellations only when those figures are available or explicitly assumed. Shift customer receipts and supplier payments to the months they occur. Track stock purchases as cash payments and stock sold as a cost in the profit forecast, once each in its respective schedule. State the treatment of taxes and debt where relevant and known. Keep a simple cash schedule even when profit forecast inputs are incomplete, leaving affected totals open where necessary.
3. **Show scenarios.** Use the founder’s ranges or evidence-backed assumptions for cautious, base, and optimistic scenarios. State what changed in each scenario and flag demand estimates above stated capacity. Include the opening cash, lowest projected cash balance, and any funding gap when enough figures are available. Treat unconfirmed funding as an explicitly named scenario.
4. **Generate the plan.** Give the model the answers, computed tables, and reviewed competitor evidence. Return the executive summary, customer and offer, competitors and positioning, pricing and sales strategy, team and operations, 90-day milestones, risks, and a 12-month financial outlook where supported. For lenders and investors, include a funding request and use of funds when supplied, and flag any additional forecast or historical documents the user needs. Link factual claims to their sources and mark all assumptions. Illustrative scenarios require explicit labels and traceable inputs.

## 5. Self-hosted LLMs to try

These models have downloadable weights under Apache 2.0. The hosting platform meters compute separately.

| Model | Suggested role | First hosting setup to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Qwen3.5-9B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B) | Starting model for cleaning up questionnaire answers and drafting a short plan. | Hugging Face ZeroGPU in a Gradio Space, using the default 48 GB GPU allocation. |
| [Qwen3.8-27B-FP8](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B-FP8) | First model to test for a detailed plan that compares several competitors and explains financial assumptions. | Modal with vLLM on a 48 GB L40S. Start with a modest context limit and one concurrent request, then measure memory and latency. The published checkpoint is about 31 GB on disk. |
| [gpt-oss-20b](https://huggingface.co/openai/gpt-oss-20b) | Lower-memory alternative to test for full-plan generation. The official weights are already quantized to MXFP4. | Try a 24 GB Modal L4 with a compatible vLLM build and a short context. Measure actual GPU memory before increasing context or concurrency. |

**For a pilot with no additional GPU spend**, start with Qwen3.5-9B on ZeroGPU. Eligible free personal accounts can host up to two ZeroGPU Spaces. Usage runs through a shared Gradio queue and consumes the calling account’s quota. Hugging Face lists 5 GPU minutes per day for free accounts and 40 for PRO at the default GPU size. Start with a short plan and measure quota used per successful draft before deciding how many users you can support.

**For a backend used by customers**, test Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 on Modal with an authenticated endpoint that scales to zero. Modal Starter includes US$30 per month of eligible compute for self-deployed workloads. Usage beyond included compute can be billed. Set limits on plan generations during testing and record GPU, CPU, and memory costs per completed plan.

Keep registry parsing and financial calculations in application code. Send the model the founder’s answers, the calculated figures, and compact, reviewed competitor records with source IDs. Have it return a structured draft with references to those IDs, then validate the references and figures before rendering the plan. Where financials are unavailable, display the gaps alongside the comparison.

Test the three models using the same cases: an idea-stage founder with few numbers, a service business with a capacity limit, a subscription business with cancellations, and a business whose competitor filings omit key figures. Compare how often the models preserve figures and citations, label assumptions correctly, and finish within the expected time and quota. These are candidates to test. Their speed, memory use, and plan quality still need to be measured on Mr. Mentor inputs.
